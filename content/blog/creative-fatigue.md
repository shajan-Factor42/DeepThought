## What is ad fatigue?

Ad fatigue is what happens when the same people see the same ad so many times that they stop responding to it. Clicks slow down, the platform has to spend more to get each lead, and your cost per result creeps up.

Nothing is broken. The campaign is still running, the targeting is still right, the budget is still spending. The ad has simply worn out with the people who keep seeing it.

For a small business this happens faster than most people expect. If you serve one county or a 20-mile radius, your audience is a few thousand people, not millions. Run one ad for two months and many of them will have seen it a dozen times.

## How to spot ad fatigue in your numbers

Ad fatigue has a clear signature. Look at these four numbers over the last four to six weeks, not day by day:

| Number | What it does when an ad is worn out |
|---|---|
| **Frequency** (how often the average person has seen the ad) | Goes up |
| **Click-through rate** | Goes down |
| **Cost per lead or cost per result** | Goes up |
| **Conversion rate on your website** | Stays about the same |

That last line matters most. If people who do click still call or fill in the form at the usual rate, your website and offer are fine. The problem is that fewer people are clicking in the first place.

On Facebook and Instagram, Ads Manager shows frequency in the reporting columns, and it can flag an ad in the Delivery column when its performance is dropping because people have seen it too often. In Google's responsive search ads, each headline and description gets a rating of Low, Good or Best. A row of Low ratings is a sign your copy has gone stale.

## Why it gets blamed on the platform

When costs rise slowly, the usual explanations are "Facebook got more expensive", "the algorithm changed" or "this channel stopped working". Sometimes that's true. More often, the same tired ad has been running for three months.

This is expensive. Owners cut a channel that was working, or an agency changes the targeting and the budget split when the real fix was a new ad. Changing the settings resets what the campaign has learned, so costs often get worse before they get better.

## Ad fatigue or something else? A quick check

Before you refresh anything, rule out the other usual suspects.

- **Costs jumped overnight.** Fatigue is gradual. A sudden jump points to a settings change, a tracking break or a new competitor bidding in your area.
- **Your website conversion rate dropped too.** Then the problem is after the click: the landing page, the offer, the phone not being answered, or a form that broke.
- **Clicks dropped but frequency is low.** Then people aren't seeing the ad enough to be tired of it. Look at the budget, the bid or the audience size.
- **Costs crept up over three to six weeks while frequency climbed.** That's ad fatigue.

## How to fix ad fatigue

**1. Refresh inside the same campaign.** Add new ads to the campaign or ad set that's already running, let them run alongside the old ones for a week or two, then pause the weakest. Starting a new campaign throws away the learning you've already paid for.

**2. Change the hook, not just the picture.** A new photo with the same headline often looks like the same ad to someone scrolling past. The first line, the offer or the angle is what makes people stop. For a home services company, try rotating angles like these:
- **The problem:** "AC making that noise again?"
- **The offer:** "$79 tune-up, this month only."
- **Proof:** a real review from a customer in your town.
- **The person:** a short video of the owner or a technician.
- **The season:** "First cold night is coming. Is your heat ready?"

**3. Keep three to five ads running at once.** With several ads in rotation, no single one wears out as fast, and you can see which angles your customers respond to.

**4. Use the season.** For local businesses, seasonal and offer-based changes do most of the work: summer cooling, fall tune-ups, holiday hours, a January promotion. Each one gives you a natural reason for a new ad.

**5. Widen the audience carefully.** If your service area allows it, a slightly bigger radius or a broader audience gives the same ad more fresh eyes. Don't stretch past the area you can actually serve.

## How often should you refresh ad creative?

There's no single right number, but these are sensible starting points for a small local business:

| Channel | Starting refresh pace |
|---|---|
| **Facebook and Instagram** | New creative every 4–6 weeks, sooner if frequency climbs fast |
| **Display and retargeting** | Every 4–8 weeks. Retargeting audiences are small and tire quickly. |
| **Streaming TV and video** | Every 2–3 months, or with each season |
| **Google search ads** | Review every 2–3 months; replace headlines rated Low |

Put these dates in the calendar. Refreshing on a schedule costs far less than waiting until the numbers hurt and then reacting. We made the same point in [Your best ad has an expiry date](blog-ad-fatigue.html).
